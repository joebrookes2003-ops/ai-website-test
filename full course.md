# Brookes AI
## The Complete Beginner's Course

*7 modules · 34 lessons · Written by Joe Brookes*

---

### How to use this course

Every lesson is short. Most take five minutes to read and five minutes to do. There's no prize for rushing.

Each one ends with a **Try this** — an actual thing to go and do. Do it. Reading about this is nearly useless; the whole skill is in the doing, and it's a much smaller skill than it looks.

Everything in a grey box is meant to be copied. Type it or paste it straight in, then change the details to yours.

**A note on which app.** I'll say ChatGPT throughout, because it's the one most people have. Every single thing here works exactly the same on Claude or Gemini. Nothing is wasted if you switch.

**If you haven't done the free course**, do that first. It gets you set up and takes half an hour. This one assumes you've got an account and you've sent a few messages.

# MODULE 1
# Getting confident

*Four lessons. The habits that separate people who find this useful from people who try it twice and give up.*

---

## 1.1 — Say more, not less

Almost everyone gets this wrong at the start, and it's the single reason most people decide AI is overrated.

Twenty-five years of search engines taught you to type as few words as possible. *Flights Malaga.* *Knee pain.* *Best washing machine.* You learned to strip everything out because that's what got results.

Here, that instinct actively works against you.

The more you say, the better the answer. Not slightly better — dramatically better. Context is the whole game.

**The thin version:**

> Something for dinner

**The version that actually helps:**

> I need something for dinner tonight. There's two of us, I've got chicken thighs, potatoes and half a cabbage. I'm a nervous cook and I don't want anything with more than about six steps. My wife doesn't eat anything spicy. About 45 minutes is fine.

Same question. Completely different answer. The second one gets you something you'll actually cook.

**What's worth including, most of the time:**

- **Who you are** — as relevant. "I'm 63." "I've never done this before." "I'm a retired nurse."
- **The situation** — the actual details, not a summary of them
- **What you want out of it** — a decision, a draft, an explanation, a list
- **Any limits** — time, money, ability, things to avoid
- **How you want it** — short, plain, formal, funny, step by step

You don't need all five every time. But when an answer disappoints you, the reason is almost always that you left three of them out.

> **Try this.** Ask it something you'd normally search for — in one short line. Then ask it again with five sentences of context. Read both. The gap between them is the whole course in one exercise.

---

## 1.2 — It's a conversation, not a slot machine

The second habit. Most people type a question, read the answer, and stop — as though they get one go.

You don't. You get as many as you like, and the good stuff is nearly always in the third or fourth reply, not the first.

Think of the first answer as a starting point. Something to react to. It's much easier to say "no, more like this" than to explain perfectly from a standing start — and you're allowed to work that way.

**What following up looks like in practice:**

> That's too long. Half it.

> The second idea is the interesting one. Ignore the rest and go into that.

> I should have said — she's vegetarian.

> Why did you suggest that one?

> Give me three more, but different from these.

Plain English. No special words. You're just talking.

**The thing that makes this work:** it remembers everything in the current conversation. You never need to repeat yourself. If you told it in message one that you've got a bad back, it still knows in message nine.

**And when to start fresh instead.** If you've wandered a long way from where you began, or it's got stuck on a wrong idea and keeps circling back to it, open a new chat. It's a clean slate. Sometimes that's faster than arguing.

> **Try this.** Ask it to plan a weekend away somewhere you'd genuinely like to go. Then send at least four follow-ups, changing your mind as you go. Notice that you never once had to re-explain the trip.

---

## 1.3 — When it gets it wrong

It will get things wrong. Regularly. This isn't a fault you've caused and it doesn't mean you've asked badly.

What matters is that you can fix it in about four seconds, and most people don't realise that.

**Just say what's wrong.**

> No, that's not right. I said the letter was from the council, not the landlord.

> You've made that up — there's no such form. Try again.

> That's not what I asked for. I wanted a list, not an essay.

It'll adjust. You don't need to be polite about it, though there's no harm in being polite if that's your nature. You certainly don't need to start again.

**Three things that go wrong most often, and the fix:**

**It's too long.** It defaults to windy. Say *"shorter"* or *"three sentences maximum."* You may need to say it twice.

**It's gone generic.** Vague, obvious, could-apply-to-anyone answers usually mean it doesn't have enough to work with. Give it more detail rather than asking again.

**It's stuck.** Occasionally it'll keep giving you variations of the same wrong thing. Don't fight it — open a new chat and start again with better wording. Takes thirty seconds and saves five minutes.

**One thing worth knowing:** you cannot break it. There is no wrong button. Nothing you type will damage anything or cause offence or get you into trouble. This is worth internalising if you're the sort of person who's cautious with technology — the usual anxiety about breaking something simply doesn't apply here.

> **Try this.** Ask it something, then deliberately tell it the answer's wrong even if it isn't. Watch what happens. Two things to notice: how easily it changes its mind, and that nothing bad happened.

---

## 1.4 — Getting things explained at your level

This is quietly one of the most useful things it does, and it costs nothing to ask.

You can set the level. Any level. And you can change it mid-conversation without embarrassment, because there's nobody there to be embarrassed in front of.

> Explain how a heat pump works. Assume I know nothing about heating systems.

> That was still too technical. Explain it like you're talking to someone at a bus stop.

> Now the version for someone who's actually thinking of buying one.

Same subject, three different explanations, thirty seconds.

**Phrases that reliably work:**

- *"Explain it like I'm completely new to this"*
- *"No jargon at all"*
- *"Use an everyday comparison"*
- *"I understood most of that except the bit about X — go over that again"*
- *"Give me the short version first, then the detail"*

**And the one most people never think to use:**

> I don't understand the answer you just gave me. Explain the explanation.

That's allowed. It's not a stupid thing to ask. It's the single fastest way to get unstuck, and there's no one to judge you for it — which is, when you think about it, the actual point of the thing.

> **Try this.** Pick something you've quietly never understood. Compound interest. What a pension actually is. How the internet works. Bitcoin. Ask for it plainly, then keep saying "simpler" until it clicks.

# MODULE 2
# Staying safe, properly

*Five lessons. The honest version — what actually happens to what you type, what can go wrong, and how to spot it.*

---

## 2.1 — What happens to what you type

Reasonable question, rarely answered straight. Here's the straight version.

**Where it goes.** What you type is sent to the company's computers and processed there. It doesn't stay on your phone. It's stored, and it's attached to your account.

**Who might see it.** Not routinely. But staff can access conversations in some circumstances — safety reviews, abuse investigations, occasionally quality checks. Assume a human *could* read it, even though almost certainly nobody will.

**Whether it's used for training.** On free accounts, often yes by default — your conversations may be used to improve the system. This is usually something you can turn off in settings, and it's worth going and looking. Search the settings for *data* or *training*.

**How long it's kept.** Varies, and it changes. Assume indefinitely unless you delete conversations yourself, which you can do.

**What this means practically.** Not that you should be frightened of it. It means treat it like a work computer rather than a diary. Most of what you'll do — letters, recipes, explanations, planning — carries no risk at all. A small category of things does. Next lesson covers exactly where that line sits.

> **Try this.** Go into settings on your account and find the data controls. Have a look at what's on and what's off. You don't have to change anything — just know it's there.

---

## 2.2 — The postcard rule

The rule, in one line: **don't type anything you wouldn't write on a postcard.**

**Never:**

- Bank account numbers, sort codes, card numbers, PINs
- Passwords, of any kind, for anything
- National Insurance number
- Passport or driving licence numbers
- Anyone else's private details without them knowing

**Think twice about:**

- Detailed medical history, particularly anything you'd not want linked to your name
- Anything about someone else's health, finances or relationships
- Confidential material from work
- Legal matters that are actually ongoing

**Perfectly fine:**

- Your age, roughly where you live, your general situation
- What you're worried about, what you want, what you're planning
- Documents with the identifying bits taken out
- Nearly everything else

**The technique that solves most of it.** When you want help with a real document, take the details out before you paste it. Replace them with something obvious:

> I've had this letter. I've replaced my account number and address with XXXX. What is it actually asking me to do, and is there a deadline?
>
> [paste the letter with the personal bits removed]

It doesn't need your real account number to tell you what the letter means. It genuinely doesn't. Two minutes with a pen or a keyboard removes nearly all the risk.

**One more.** If you're helping someone else — a parent, a neighbour — the same rule applies, and it's their information, not yours. Ask first.

> **Try this.** Find a letter with your details on it. Copy the text out, replacing anything identifying with XXXX. Paste it in and ask what it means. You'll see how little it actually needed.

---

## 2.3 — Spotting a made-up answer

This is the most important lesson in the course.

It makes things up. Not occasionally — regularly. Names, dates, statistics, laws, phone numbers, entire books that don't exist, court cases that never happened. And the problem isn't that it makes things up. The problem is **it sounds exactly the same when it does.**

There's no wobble in the voice. No hedging. A completely fabricated statistic arrives with the same calm confidence as a true one. Every instinct you've developed for detecting when a person is unsure is useless here, because it isn't a person and it isn't unsure — it doesn't know that it doesn't know.

**Where it's most likely to invent things:**

- Specific numbers and statistics
- Dates and historical details
- Names of people, books, studies, articles
- Legal rules, benefit entitlements, tax thresholds
- Anything very recent
- Anything obscure or specialised
- Phone numbers and addresses — it will happily invent a plausible-looking one

**Where it's usually reliable:**

- Explaining a concept you're trying to grasp
- Rewriting or summarising something you gave it
- Ideas, suggestions, options
- General how-things-work knowledge
- Anything where being slightly off doesn't matter

**The rule that covers it:**

> **If getting it wrong would cost you money, health, or time — check it somewhere else.**

Not everything. Nothing bad happens if a recipe's a bit odd or a holiday suggestion is mediocre. But a benefit rule, a tax threshold, a drug interaction, a legal deadline — check.

**Two questions that help:**

> How confident are you about that, and which parts should I double-check?

> Where does that come from? Give me the source.

Both are useful. Neither is a guarantee — it can invent a source as easily as a fact. If it names one, go and look at the source yourself.

**And the practical test:** if you're about to act on something, ask yourself where you'd have got this information five years ago. Then go there and check.

> **Try this.** Ask it something specific about a subject you know very well — your old job, your town, a hobby. Read the answer as an expert. Most people find one or two things quietly wrong, and it's a useful thing to see with your own eyes.

---

## 2.4 — It agrees with you too easily

A subtler problem, and one people rarely get warned about.

It's built to be agreeable. That sounds harmless. In practice it means:

- Tell it your plan, and it'll usually tell you it's a good plan
- Push back on a correct answer, and it'll often cave and agree with you
- Ask "is this a good idea?" and you'll get a yes far more often than you should

So it's a poor judge of your ideas, and a genuinely bad substitute for a friend who'd tell you the truth. If you go to it for reassurance, you will get reassurance — whether or not you should.

**How to get a real answer.** You have to ask for the opposite explicitly:

> Give me the strongest argument against this.

> What am I not thinking of?

> Be critical. Don't be encouraging. What's wrong with this plan?

> Argue the other side properly.

> If this goes badly, what will have caused it?

The difference in usefulness is substantial. Try both on the same question sometime.

**Where this matters most.** Anything where you're half-hoping for a particular answer. Big purchases. Whether to have the difficult conversation. Whether the business idea's any good. Whether you're right in the argument. Those are exactly the times it'll tell you what you want to hear, and exactly the times you need something better.

**And the serious version.** It is not a doctor, a solicitor, or a friend. It's useful for understanding what your doctor told you. It is not useful for deciding whether to go and see one — because if you're looking for permission not to, it'll very likely give it to you.

> **Try this.** Think of something you're considering. Ask "is this a good idea?" and read the answer. Then start a new chat and ask "give me the strongest argument against this." Compare.

---

## 2.5 — Scams, fakes, and what you never need to pay for

This audience gets targeted. Worth ten minutes.

**Fake apps.** Search any app store for AI and you'll find dozens of copies with similar names and near-identical logos, many charging a weekly subscription for something that's free elsewhere. Check the developer name before installing:

- ChatGPT → **OpenAI**
- Claude → **Anthropic**
- Gemini → **Google**

If it doesn't say that, it isn't the real one.

**Fake websites.** Type the address in yourself. Don't search for it and click the first result, because the top results are often paid adverts and some of them are impersonations. The real addresses are chatgpt.com, claude.ai, gemini.google.com.

**Phishing.** If you get an email or text asking you to sign in to your AI account, verify something, or claim a free upgrade — don't click it. Same rule as your bank. Go to the app directly.

**Paid courses and "secret prompts."** There's a large industry selling AI knowledge that's freely available. Be sceptical of anything promising income, secrets, or urgency. (I'm aware of the irony of writing that in a paid course. The test is whether it's teaching you something useful — and whether you were given enough for free to judge.)

**What you never need to pay for.** All three main assistants are free to use. The paid tiers give you more capacity and some extra features. For everything in this course, **the free version is enough.** Don't let anyone tell you otherwise, including the apps themselves when they prompt you to upgrade.

**AI-generated scams generally.** Worth knowing, since it's the same technology: voice cloning is now good enough that a short clip can imitate someone's voice convincingly. If you get a distressed call from a family member asking for money urgently, hang up and ring them back on the number you already have. Agree a family password if it'd put your mind at rest. This is the single most common way this technology is currently used against people of any age.

> **Try this.** Search your app store for "AI assistant" and look at the results. Count how many are copies. It's a useful five minutes.

# MODULE 3
# Writing things

*Six lessons. The module people tell me changes things — because it's not about writing, it's about the things you've been putting off.*

---

## 3.1 — The four-part recipe

Every piece of writing you'll ever ask for needs the same four things. Learn this once and the rest of the module is variations.

**One — who you are and what happened.** The actual facts. Dates, amounts, what was said, what wasn't done. More detail than feels necessary.

**Two — what you want to happen.** Be specific. Not "sort this out" — a refund, a date in writing, an apology, a call back by Friday.

**Three — the tone.** The part people leave out, and the part that matters most. Firm. Warm. Formal. Apologetic. Friendly but clear. Without this you get call-centre prose.

**Four — how long.** Short. One paragraph. Under 200 words. Left alone it will waffle.

**Put together:**

> I ordered a sofa on 3rd March. I was given a delivery date of 20th April. It's now 2nd June and I've heard nothing. I've phoned three times and been promised a call back each time. Nobody has called. I've paid £1,200.
>
> I want either a firm delivery date in writing within seven days, or a full refund.
>
> Write this as an email. Firm and serious, but not aggressive — I don't want to sound like I'm ranting. Short enough that someone will read the whole thing.

That's the recipe. Everything else in this module is that, applied.

**And the rule that matters as much:** never send the first version. It's a draft. Read it, find the bits that aren't you, and say so.

> **Try this.** Write out the four parts for something real before you type anything. Then send it as one message. You'll notice how much better the answer is than when you improvise.

---

## 3.2 — Complaints that actually get results

Complaints are where this earns its money, because most people either don't complain or complain badly — too angry, too vague, or too apologetic to be taken seriously.

**What makes a complaint work,** and what to tell it to include:

- **A clear timeline** with dates. Vagueness is what lets companies stall.
- **The money involved**, stated plainly.
- **What you've already tried.** Calls made, promises given, nothing done.
- **One specific request.** Not a list of grievances — one thing you want.
- **A deadline.** Fourteen days is standard and reasonable.
- **What you'll do next.** The ombudsman, the regulator, a chargeback, small claims.

That last one is the lever, and most people don't know to use it. Ask:

> Who regulates this industry in the UK, and what's the escalation route if they don't respond? Mention it in the email so they know I know.

**A word of caution.** It may name a specific ombudsman, quote a specific law, or cite a specific right. **Check that before you send it.** Getting the regulator wrong makes you look like you don't know what you're doing, which is precisely the opposite of what you want. A quick search will confirm it.

**Escalating.** If the first email gets nowhere:

> They've ignored this for 14 days. Write a follow-up. Firmer. Reference the original email and date. Make clear this is my final attempt before I escalate.

**And the version people forget:**

> Write me a short, warm thank-you email to a company whose staff member went out of their way for me. I'd like it to reach their manager.

Takes ninety seconds and does more good than the complaints.

> **Try this.** Think of a complaint you gave up on. Give it the four parts plus a timeline and a deadline. See what comes back.

---

## 3.3 — The emails you've been putting off

Not the difficult ones — those are next. These are the ones that are just *effortful*. The ones that sit on the list for weeks for no good reason.

Chasing a tradesman. Replying to something from three weeks ago. Cancelling something. Asking for a quote. Getting back to someone about a date.

**The trick with these is to be brief and stop overthinking:**

> Short, friendly email to a builder who quoted me in April and hasn't been in touch since. I still want the work done. Two sentences, no fuss.

> I need to cancel a gym membership. Polite, no explanation needed, just clear.

> Reply to a friend who invited us to something on the 14th. We can't go, we're away. Warm, sorry to miss it, suggest another time.

**The apology-for-being-slow email**, which everyone needs and nobody wants to write:

> I've left an email three weeks without replying. Write a short reply that acknowledges the delay without grovelling about it, and gets straight to the point.

The instruction *without grovelling* is doing real work there. Left to itself it produces something excessively apologetic that makes the delay seem worse than it was.

**Batch them.** Sit down with the list of things you've been avoiding and do six in twenty minutes. It's the single most satisfying use of this thing, and it's the one that makes people believers.

> **Try this.** Write down three emails you've been avoiding. Do all three now. Time yourself.

---

## 3.4 — Difficult personal messages

The harder category. Family, friends, neighbours, things that have gone wrong between people.

**A caveat first.** Don't outsource these. If the words are entirely not yours, the person will feel it, even if they can't say why. What this is good for is getting you *started*, and stopping you sending the version you'd regret.

**Where it genuinely helps:**

**Getting the tone down.** You've written something angry. Before you send it:

> Here's a message I've written. I'm upset and I think it shows. Tell me honestly how it'll land, and give me a version that says the same thing without the edge.
>
> [paste it]

That's arguably the best use of the whole technology. It's a second opinion at eleven o'clock at night when there's nobody to ask.

**Saying no.** Genuinely difficult for a lot of people:

> I need to tell my sister we can't host Christmas this year. I don't want to give a long explanation and I don't want a row. Warm but clear. Short.

**Raising something awkward.** A neighbour's hedge, a friend who owes money, a relative who's been unfair:

> Help me raise something with a neighbour without it becoming a dispute. The situation is [x]. I want to stay on good terms — we've got to live next door to each other.

**Understanding the other side.** Underrated:

> Here's what she sent me. I've read it as hostile. Is there another way to read it?

Sometimes the answer is no, it's hostile. Often it isn't, and you've saved yourself a fortnight.

**Where it doesn't help.** Grief, love, apology that needs to be real. Write those yourself, badly. Badly and yours beats polished and borrowed, every time.

> **Try this.** Find a message you sent when annoyed. Paste it in and ask how it landed. Instructive, if slightly uncomfortable.

---

## 3.5 — Cards, speeches and condolences

The writing that arrives with a deadline and a blank mind.

**Cards.** Give it something to work with and it's good; give it nothing and it's a greetings card cliché.

> 80th birthday card for my uncle. He was a fisherman for forty years, he's very dry, he'd hate anything sentimental. Three or four lines. Make it warm but let him keep his dignity.

**Speeches.** Father of the bride, retirement, a big birthday. Work in stages:

> I'm speaking at my daughter's wedding. Five minutes. I'm not a confident speaker. Before you write anything — ask me questions that'll help you understand her and get the stories out of me.

Asking it to interview you first is the technique most people never discover, and it's much better than describing everything up front.

Then:

> Now draft it. Warm, a bit funny, not a stand-up routine. One moment where it goes quiet.

Then read it aloud and cut everything that doesn't sound like you.

**Condolences.** Handle with care.

Use it to get past the paralysis, not to write the letter. The instruction that helps:

> I need to write to a friend whose husband has died. They were married 51 years. I keep starting and stopping. Give me two or three honest openings — nothing that sounds like a card, no clichés about better places.

Then write the rest yourself. It only takes the first sentence to unstick you, and the rest should be your voice. That letter will be kept for years. It should be from you.

**Eulogies.** Same approach, but let it help with structure — what order to put things in, where to put the difficult bit. Structure is genuinely hard when you're grieving. The words should still be yours.

> **Try this.** Next card you write, give it three specific details about the person. See the difference specificity makes.

---

## 3.6 — Making it sound like you

The complaint people make: *it doesn't sound like me.* Fixable, and worth fixing.

**Why it happens.** Left alone it writes in a particular way — slightly formal, slightly over-eager, fond of certain words. Everyone who uses it produces the same texture, which is why you can increasingly spot it.

**Words to strike out.** These give it away, and most people don't want them:

*delve, tapestry, navigate, landscape, testament, robust, leverage, journey, resonate, elevate, unlock, embark, crucial, moreover, furthermore, it's important to note*

You can simply ban them:

> Never use the words delve, journey, tapestry, robust, navigate or resonate. Don't start sentences with "moreover" or "furthermore."

**Other giveaways to remove:**

- Three-part lists everywhere. Real people don't structure everything in threes.
- "It's not just X, it's Y." Very common, very recognisable.
- Openings that restate your question before answering.
- Excessive enthusiasm about ordinary things.

**The instruction that fixes most of it:**

> Write it the way a normal person would write it. Plain words. Short sentences. Some sentences can be very short. Don't be enthusiastic. Don't try to be clever.

**The proper fix — give it a sample.** This is much more effective than describing your style:

> Here are three emails I've written. Study how I write — sentence length, how formal I am, what I do and don't do. Then write the new one in that voice.
>
> [paste three things you've written]

Do this once, keep the conversation, reuse it. It's the closest thing to a genuine trick in this course.

**And the last check, always:** read it aloud. If you'd never say it, change it. Your ear is better than any rule.

> **Try this.** Paste in three of your own emails and ask it to describe your writing style. Reading its description of how you write is oddly revealing.

# MODULE 4
# Understanding things

*Five lessons. For every letter, form and document that arrives and doesn't quite make sense.*

---

## 4.1 — The question that decodes anything

One question does most of the work:

> **What is this actually asking me to do, and is there a deadline?**

Paste in any letter, form, contract or notice, ask that, and you'll get further in ten seconds than in ten minutes of reading it yourself.

**The fuller version, worth saving:**

> Here's a document. Take out anything identifying — I've replaced my details with XXXX. Tell me:
>
> 1. What is this, in one sentence
> 2. What does it want me to do
> 3. Is there a deadline
> 4. What happens if I ignore it
> 5. Is there anything in here I should be concerned about
> 6. Anything unusual, or that I should query
>
> [paste the document]

Point six is the valuable one. It's the question you don't know to ask.

**How to get a document in there.** Three ways:

- **Type it out** — fine for short letters
- **Copy and paste** — if it's already an email or a PDF you can select text in
- **Photograph it** — the apps take photos. Take a picture of the letter, upload it, ask your question. Works well, and it's the easiest route for most people.

**Always remove the identifying details first.** Account numbers, reference numbers, your address. It doesn't need them, and the postcard rule applies.

> **Try this.** Find something in the pile that you've not properly read. Photograph it, ask the six questions.

---

## 4.2 — Letters from official places

Councils, HMRC, DWP, utilities, the NHS. Written in a register that seems designed to be unclear.

**Start with the six questions from the last lesson.** Then go further:

> Is this a bill, a demand, a notice, or just information?

> Is this saying I owe money, or that I might?

> Explain the calculation. Where do these numbers come from?

> Is this a final notice or an early one? How worried should I be?

> What's my right to challenge this, and how long have I got?

**Then get the reply written:**

> Write a letter querying this. I don't understand how they've reached this figure and I want it explained. Polite, formal, and I want it to be clear I expect a proper answer.

**A serious warning.** It may tell you what the rules are — benefit rates, tax thresholds, appeal windows, entitlements. **Do not act on those without checking.** These change every year, they're exactly the sort of thing it gets wrong, and the cost of being wrong is real.

Use it to *understand* the letter. Use the official source — gov.uk, or the phone number on the letter itself — to confirm anything you're going to rely on.

**Where to check:**

- Benefits, tax, official rules: **gov.uk**
- Consumer rights and debt: **Citizens Advice**
- Anything about the letter itself: the number printed on it

**And if it's frightening.** A letter about debt, court, or eviction is worth understanding quickly, and this will do that in seconds. But then ring Citizens Advice or a free debt line. Understanding the letter is not the same as having advice, and for those letters you want a person.

> **Try this.** Take the most confusing official letter you've kept. Run the six questions. Then check any rule it quotes against gov.uk.

---

## 4.3 — Contracts, insurance and small print

The documents nobody reads. Now readable.

**For insurance:**

> Here's my policy. Tell me in plain English: what's covered, what's specifically not covered, what the excess is, and what would invalidate it. Then tell me the three things people most often get caught out by with this type of policy.

**For a contract before signing:**

> Explain this contract in plain English. Then tell me: which clauses are unusual or one-sided, what I'm agreeing to that I might not realise, how I get out of it, and what I should ask to change before signing.

That last part — *what should I ask to change* — is the one that makes it worth doing. Most people don't know negotiating is available.

**For terms and conditions:**

> Summarise these terms in ten bullet points. Focus only on things that could actually affect me. Flag anything about automatic renewal, price rises, cancellation fees, or my data.

**For comparing two things:**

> Here are two quotes. Put them side by side in a table. Show me where they differ in what's actually covered, not just price. Tell me which is better value and why.

**The limits, stated plainly.** This is not legal advice. For anything with real money or real consequence — property, employment, a dispute, anything you'd struggle to undo — see a solicitor. What this does is get you to the meeting already understanding the document, with your questions written down. That makes the meeting shorter and cheaper, which is a genuine saving.

> **Try this.** Take an insurance policy you already hold. Ask what's *not* covered. Most people find something they assumed was.

---

## 4.4 — Medical letters and leaflets

Useful, and the place to be most careful. Both things are true.

**What it's good for:**

> Here's a letter from the hospital. Explain the medical terms in plain English. What is it telling me?

> What does this test result actually measure, and what do the numbers mean in general terms?

> Here's the leaflet for a medication. What are the common side effects, as opposed to the rare and alarming ones the leaflet lists equally?

That last one is genuinely valuable. Patient leaflets list every reported side effect with no sense of proportion, which frightens people out of taking medicine they need.

**The best use of all — preparing for an appointment:**

> I'm seeing my GP about [x] on Thursday. I get flustered and forget things. Help me write down what to tell them, in a sensible order, and what to ask. One page, so I can take it with me.

Doctors like this. You get more out of ten minutes, and you don't remember the important thing in the car park.

**And afterwards:**

> My consultant said [x]. I nodded but I didn't follow it. Explain it properly.

**The hard limits. Please read these.**

- **It cannot diagnose you.** It doesn't examine you, doesn't know your history, doesn't have your notes.
- **Never change or stop medication** based on anything it says. Ever. Talk to a pharmacist — free, walk in, no appointment.
- **It is confidently wrong about medicine** as easily as anything else, and here that costs more.
- **It will reassure you** if you're seeking reassurance, because it's agreeable. That's a dangerous combination when you're worried about a symptom.

**If you're using it to talk yourself out of seeing a doctor — stop, and go and see the doctor.** That's the failure mode that matters, and it's worth being blunt about.

- Urgent: **111**
- Emergency: **999**
- Medication questions: any pharmacist

> **Try this.** Next appointment, use it to prepare a one-page note beforehand. Take it with you.

---

## 4.5 — Learning anything, at any level

Not documents — the general skill of getting something explained until it actually lands.

**Start where you are, honestly:**

> Explain how the pension system works. Assume I know nothing. No jargon. Then tell me what I should be asking about my own.

**Keep going until it clicks.** The follow-ups that work:

> Simpler.

> Give me an everyday comparison.

> I followed all of that except [x]. Just that bit again.

> Now show me how it applies to my situation, which is [x].

> What's the most common misunderstanding about this?

**Test yourself.** The thing almost nobody does:

> Ask me five questions to check whether I've actually understood that.

You'll find out quickly whether you followed it or just felt like you did. That difference is most of learning.

**Get the shape before the detail:**

> Before the detail — give me the overall map. What are the main parts and how do they fit together?

**And the two questions worth asking about anything:**

> What do people usually get wrong about this?

> If I only remember one thing, what should it be?

**The point.** You can ask the same question six times. You can admit you didn't understand. You can ask something you feel you should already know. Nobody sighs, nobody remembers, and there's no cost to it. For a lot of people that's the actual value of the whole thing — not the answers, the absence of embarrassment.

> **Try this.** Pick something you've quietly never understood and stay with it for fifteen minutes. Keep saying "simpler" or "I didn't follow that." Notice how far you get.

# MODULE 5
# Everyday life

*Six lessons. Lower stakes, and the ones that turn this into a habit rather than a tool you remember occasionally.*

---

## 5.1 — Planning a trip

Where the "say more" rule pays off most obviously.

**The thin version** gets you a guidebook. **The full version** gets you an itinerary:

> Four days in Edinburgh in September. Two of us, both early sixties. My husband can't manage a lot of walking — a mile at a time at most, and hills are difficult. We like history, good food, and we'd rather avoid anywhere crowded. We're not big drinkers. Budget is moderate — comfortable but not luxury. We'll have a car but would rather not drive in the city.
>
> Plan the four days. Realistic pace, not packed. Tell me what to book ahead.

**Then refine it:**

> Day two is too much. Cut it in half.

> What if it rains all week?

> Where's within an hour's drive that's worth a day?

> We've been to Edinburgh before and done the castle. Give me things people usually miss.

**The genuinely useful ones:**

> What do people wish they'd known before going?

> What's overrated there?

> Is September a good time, or is there a better month?

**Getting around, which is where the real anxiety is:**

> Explain how to get from the airport to the city centre. Assume I've never been. What are the options and which would you use with a suitcase?

**And the limits.** It can't book anything. It won't have live prices, availability or opening times, and it may confidently state that somewhere is open when it closed two years ago. Check anything you're relying on — opening times especially. Use it for the plan, not the facts.

> **Try this.** Plan a trip you're actually considering. Give it eight sentences of context.

---

## 5.2 — Food and shopping

The most-used thing, once people get going.

**Work from what you have,** not from a recipe you then shop for:

> Chicken thighs, a bag of potatoes, half a cabbage, some cream that needs using. Two of us. I'm not a confident cook. Under an hour. What can I make?

**Adapt anything:**

> Here's a recipe. My husband can't have dairy. Rewrite it.

> Half this — it serves six and there's two of us.

> I don't have a food processor. Is there another way?

> Can I do this in a slow cooker? What changes?

**Plan a week properly:**

> Meal plan for the week. Two of us. Nothing complicated on weeknights. I want to cook once on Sunday and eat off it Monday and Tuesday. One thing I've never made before. Then a shopping list, sorted by supermarket aisle.

The aisle-sorted list is a small thing that people love.

**Cut waste:**

> What can I make with what's about to go off? I've got [list].

**Learn as you go:**

> Why does the recipe say to rest the meat? What actually happens if I don't?

**The one caution.** It's good on cooking and unreliable on nutrition — it will state calorie counts and nutritional figures with total confidence and they're often wrong. Don't use it for anything you're tracking, and don't use it for anything medical about diet.

> **Try this.** Open the fridge. List what's in it. Ask what you can make. Cook it.

---

## 5.3 — Gifts and occasions

Small, but it removes a recurring low-level dread.

**Gifts — detail is everything:**

> Present for my brother-in-law, 70th birthday. Retired postman, keen gardener, does the crossword every day, hates fuss and says he doesn't want anything. Around £40. He's got every gardening gadget going.

The last sentence is the useful one — telling it what *won't* work rules out the obvious.

**Follow up:**

> Nothing that needs assembling.

> More unusual — those are all quite predictable.

> Something we could do rather than buy.

**Occasions:**

> Planning a 40th anniversary lunch. Twelve people, ages 8 to 91. Someone's coeliac, two are vegetarian. Half will want to leave by three. Help me think it through — what am I forgetting?

*What am I forgetting* is worth attaching to any plan you make.

**Cards, invitations, thank-yous** — see Module 3. Same recipe.

**Difficult occasions too.** First Christmas after a bereavement, a birthday nobody knows how to handle. It's willing to think about these with you at an hour when you don't want to ring anyone.

> **Try this.** Next gift you're stuck on, give it six specific details about the person, including what they'd hate.

---

## 5.4 — Hobbies and taking something up

Good at getting you started on something new, and at the bit where you get stuck and there's nobody to ask.

**Starting:**

> I'd like to learn watercolours. Complete beginner, 66, reasonable patience, no natural talent that I'm aware of. What do I actually need to buy — the minimum, not the ideal? And what should I do in the first month?

The *minimum, not the ideal* instruction saves money. It defaults to recommending the full kit.

**Getting unstuck:**

> My tomatoes have yellow leaves on the bottom, going upwards. Pots, greenhouse, watered every other day. What's likely wrong?

> I've made the same mistake four times in this knitting pattern. Here's what it says and here's what keeps happening.

**Going deeper:**

> I've been gardening thirty years but I've never understood soil pH properly. Explain it at the level of someone who knows plants but not chemistry.

**Practising:**

> Give me a small exercise for this week. Fifteen minutes a day, something that'll actually improve my drawing rather than just producing a picture.

**Things you'd feel daft asking a person:**

> Everyone in my chess club uses this term. What does it mean? I've been nodding for two years.

That's a real use case and worth naming out loud.

> **Try this.** Pick something you've meant to take up for years. Ask for a first month.

---

## 5.5 — Helping grandchildren

Common request, and worth doing carefully.

**Do it for them and you've done them no favours.** Homework written by AI teaches nothing, and schools are increasingly able to spot it. The point is to help them think, not to produce the work.

**The right way to use it — for you:**

> My granddaughter is 12 and doing algebra. I've forgotten all of it. Explain what she's likely learning so I can help without embarrassing myself.

That's excellent. You're preparing, not substituting.

**Getting it to teach rather than answer:**

> Here's the maths problem. Don't give the answer. Give me hints I can offer, one at a time, so she works it out herself.

**Explaining at the right age:**

> Explain photosynthesis to a nine-year-old. Then give me a question to ask her afterwards to check she's got it.

**Things to do together:**

> Rainy Saturday, grandchildren aged 6 and 9, in a small flat, no special equipment. Five things we could do that don't involve a screen.

**And the conversation worth having.** They're growing up with this. If you understand it a bit — that it makes things up, that it agrees too easily, that it isn't a friend — you're able to have that conversation with them. Which is more useful to them than any homework help.

> **Try this.** Ask it to explain something they're learning, at your level. Then ask for one good question to ask them about it.

---

## 5.6 — Idle questions and settling arguments

The most enjoyable use, and the one that builds the habit.

**The questions that used to go unanswered:**

> Why do we drive on the left and most of Europe doesn't?

> What actually happens in a hospital when you go in through A&E?

> Why is Wednesday spelled like that?

**Following the thread**, which is the pleasure of it:

> That's interesting. Go on.

> How do we know that?

> What happened to him afterwards?

**Settling arguments — with a warning.** Perfectly good for whether it was 1974 or 1975. But remember it makes things up, and if it agrees with you that proves nothing at all. For anything you're properly betting on, check it.

**Things you've half-forgotten:**

> There's a film from the eighties, something about a submarine, I think Sean Connery was in it. What am I thinking of?

Surprisingly good at this.

**And the deeper ones,** which people are often surprised are available:

> I've been thinking about why I gave up music at 20. Ask me some questions about it.

It's a reasonable thinking partner. It is not a therapist, it agrees too readily, and if something is genuinely weighing on you, talk to a person. But for turning something over on a quiet afternoon, it's better than nothing and often better than that.

> **Try this.** Ask the question you've wondered about for years and never bothered to look up. Then ask three follow-ups.

# MODULE 6
# Work and money

*Five lessons. If you're still working, looking for work, or running something of your own. Skip it if none of that applies — nothing later depends on it.*

---

## 6.1 — Your CV

Most people's CVs are worse than their careers. This fixes that unusually well.

**Start by getting it looked at:**

> Here's my CV. I'm applying for [type of role]. Be critical, not encouraging. What's weak, what's missing, what would put someone off, and what's the first thing you'd change?
>
> [paste it]

*Be critical, not encouraging* matters here. Without it you'll be told your CV is strong, and it probably isn't.

**Turn duties into achievements.** The commonest failing:

> Here's a bullet point from my CV: "Responsible for managing the team." Rewrite it as an achievement. Ask me whatever you need to know to make it specific.

Letting it ask you questions is the move. It'll pull out numbers and outcomes you'd never have thought to include.

**Tailor it to the job:**

> Here's my CV and here's the job advert. Which of my experience matters most for this one, and how should I reorder things? What am I not making enough of?

**If you've been out of work,** or you're older and worried about it:

> I'm 58 and applying for roles where I'll likely be the oldest applicant. How should I present 30 years of experience so it reads as valuable rather than as a reason I'll be expensive or set in my ways?

Direct question, useful answer.

**Two rules.** Never let it invent anything — every claim must be true, and it will happily embellish if you don't watch. And read the final version aloud: if it doesn't sound like you, you'll be caught out at interview.

> **Try this.** Paste your CV in and ask for honest criticism. Take the first suggestion.

---

## 6.2 — Applications and covering letters

**The covering letter** is where most people struggle and where this saves the most time.

> Here's my CV and here's the job advert. Write a covering letter. Not generic — it should be obvious I've read this specific advert. Three short paragraphs. Confident but not boastful. Plain English, no business-speak.
>
> [paste both]

**Then make it yours.** Cut anything you wouldn't say. Add one specific thing about why *that* organisation. That sentence is often what gets you the interview, and it has to be real.

**Application forms** — the awkward boxes:

> The form asks: "Describe a time you handled a difficult situation." I've got an example but I can't structure it. Here's what happened: [describe it]. Put it into a clear answer in about 150 words.

**Working out whether to bother:**

> Here's the advert. I've got [x] but not [y]. Realistically, is it worth applying, and how should I handle the gap?

**And the thing nobody thinks to ask:**

> What questions should I ask about this role that most candidates wouldn't?

**One warning.** Many employers can now detect AI-written applications, and a letter that's obviously machine-produced does you harm. The safe approach: use it for structure and a first draft, then rewrite it in your own words. Never send the version it gave you.

> **Try this.** Take a real advert. Get a draft. Rewrite every sentence in your own words. Compare the two.

---

## 6.3 — Interviews

The best interview practice available, and it's free.

**Get the likely questions:**

> Here's the job advert and my CV. Give me the ten questions I'm most likely to be asked, including the difficult ones about my gaps.

**Then actually practise:**

> Interview me for this job. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer. After each one, tell me what was good and what I should change. Be honest — don't just be encouraging.

That's the exercise. It's the single most useful thing in this module, and almost nobody does it. Type your answers as you'd say them.

**Prepare the hard ones properly:**

> How do I answer "why did you leave your last job" when the truth is I was made redundant after a restructure I disagreed with?

> I've got a two-year gap for caring for my mother. How do I handle that?

**Your own questions:**

> What should I ask them that shows I've thought seriously about the role — not the obvious ones everyone asks?

**Afterwards:**

> The interview's done. Write a short follow-up email. Brief, not desperate.

**And on salary:**

> They've offered [x]. I want [y]. Help me write the reply — polite, clear, doesn't jeopardise the offer.

> **Try this.** Do the mock interview properly. Ten questions, honest feedback. It's uncomfortable and it works.

---

## 6.4 — Running something small

If you've a business, a trade, a side income, or you're thinking about it.

**The admin that eats your evenings:**

> Write a quote for a customer. Job is [x], price is [y], includes [z], excludes [a]. Professional, clear, no ambiguity about what's included — I've been caught out before.

> Polite but firm chase for an invoice that's 40 days overdue. Second reminder. I want to keep the customer if possible.

> Turn my scribbled notes from this job into a proper written record.

**Customer replies:**

> A customer's left an unfair review. Here's what they said and here's what actually happened. Write a public reply — calm, factual, no argument. Other people will read it, so it should make me look reasonable.

Genuinely valuable. The instinct is to fire back, and it stops you.

**Writing about what you do:**

> Here's what my business does. Write the description for my website. Plain English, no marketing waffle, sounds like a person not a brochure.

**Thinking things through:**

> I'm considering [decision]. Here's the situation. Give me the strongest argument against it, and tell me what I'm not thinking about.

**Where it stops.** It's not an accountant and it's not a solicitor. Tax rules, employment law, contracts with real money in them — it will state rules confidently and be wrong. Use it to understand and to prepare. Then use a professional for anything that counts.

> **Try this.** Take a piece of admin you've been putting off. Do it in ten minutes.

---

## 6.5 — Making something of what you know

You have decades of knowledge. This lowers the effort of doing something with it.

**Work out what you've got:**

> I worked in [field] for 30 years. Ask me questions about what I did, then tell me what parts of it other people would find valuable — including things I probably take for granted.

The last clause matters. What's obvious to you after thirty years is what other people don't know.

**Options, realistically:**

> I've got expertise in [x]. I'm 62, I want something part-time and flexible, and I'd rather not do anything that involves selling to people. What's realistic? Be honest about what's hard.

**Then get moving:**

> Help me price this. Here's what I'd be offering and here's roughly who for.

> Write the description of what I do for a website.

> I've never done this before and I feel like a fraud charging for it. Talk me through that honestly.

**A caution, and a firm one.** There is a large industry selling people the idea that they can turn their expertise into easy income online. Most of it doesn't work. Ask it directly:

> Be realistic about this. What actually happens to most people who try this, and how long does it take before anything comes of it?

It'll be more honest than the adverts, if you ask it to be. Remember it agrees too easily — if you want the truth about your idea, you have to ask for the case against.

> **Try this.** Let it interview you about your working life. Then ask what parts other people would pay to learn.

# MODULE 7
# Making it stick

*Three short lessons. What happens after the course finishes, which is where most people quietly stop.*

---

## 7.1 — Your first month

Courses don't fail because the content's wrong. They fail because nothing changes on the Monday.

**Weeks one and two — one thing a day, five minutes.** Not a project. One question, one letter, one email. The aim is only to make opening it normal.

**Weeks three and four — reach for it first.** When something comes up, try it there before doing it the old way. Some of the time it'll be slower. That's fine — you're finding the edges, and knowing where it's useless is as valuable as knowing where it's good.

**Make it easy to reach.** On your phone's home screen, first page. Bookmarked in your browser. Every extra step is a reason not to bother.

**Five things to do this month:**

1. The letter you didn't understand — ask what it's asking you to do
2. The email you've been avoiding — send it
3. Something you've never understood — keep asking until it clicks
4. A meal from what's in the fridge
5. A trip, real or imagined, planned properly

**One habit above all others:** when you catch yourself thinking *I can't be bothered with that* — that's the moment. That's the whole thing. The value isn't in doing clever work; it's in the friction disappearing from things you'd otherwise leave undone.

> **Try this.** Put it on your home screen now, before you read on.

---

## 7.2 — When it goes wrong

It will. Here's what's usually happening.

**The answers are bland and useless.** You're not giving it enough. Go back to Module 1.1 — more context, always.

**It's not doing what I asked.** Say so plainly and specifically. Don't rephrase the whole thing; just name what's wrong.

**It keeps getting it wrong the same way.** Start a new conversation. It's got stuck, and arguing costs more than beginning again.

**It's too long and too formal.** Ask for shorter, twice if needed. Add "plain words, short sentences."

**It made something up.** It does. Expected behaviour, not a malfunction. Check anything that matters — Module 2.3.

**It agreed with something I know is wrong.** Also expected — Module 2.4. Ask for the argument against instead.

**It sounds like a machine.** Module 3.6. Give it three samples of your writing.

**I don't know what to ask.** The best question when stuck:

> I'm trying to [x]. I don't really know what to ask you. What should I be asking?

**It's stopped working / it's busy.** Free accounts have limits and the services go down occasionally. Wait, or use one of the others. Nothing's broken and nothing's your fault.

> **Try this.** Next time you get a poor answer, work out which of these it was. It'll almost always be the first one.

---

## 7.3 — Where to go next

**You know enough.** That's worth saying plainly. Everything from here is practice, and practice means using it, not studying it.

**Try the other two.** Claude and Gemini work the same way. Some people find one suits them better, and it costs nothing to find out.

**Should you pay?** Paid tiers are roughly £15–20 a month. Worth it only if you're regularly hitting limits or you need it for work. For everything in this course, the free version is enough. Wait until you feel the limit rather than paying in anticipation.

**Things you'll come across:**

- **Voice.** You can talk to it rather than type. Much easier if typing is a chore, and worth trying.
- **Photos.** Point your camera at anything — a letter, a plant, a rash, a broken part — and ask about it.
- **Documents.** You can upload files and ask questions about them.
- **Web search.** It can look things up, which helps with recent information — though it can still get it wrong.

**Things to be sceptical about.** There's a large industry selling AI secrets, prompt packs, and courses promising income. Most of it repackages what you now know. You don't need it.

**The three things worth carrying:**

1. **Say more, not less.** Context is everything.
2. **Check what matters.** It's confidently wrong, and it never sounds it.
3. **It agrees too easily.** Ask for the case against.

Everything else is detail.

**And the point of all this.** You didn't learn a piece of software. You closed a gap that was making you feel behind — and that feeling was never really about the technology. There'll be another one of these along in a few years. You'll be fine with that one too, for the same reason you were fine with this one: you sat down and used it until it stopped being strange.

That's all it ever takes.
