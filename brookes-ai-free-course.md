# Lesson 1
## What this thing actually is

*About 5 minutes*

Here's the honest version, without the nonsense.

Imagine someone who has read almost everything ever written. Every book, every newspaper, every recipe, every instruction manual, every argument on the internet. All of it.

And from all that reading, they've become extremely good at one particular skill: working out what words should come next.

That's it. That's the trick.

Which sounds underwhelming when you say it out loud. It doesn't sound like it should be useful. But it turns out that if you're good enough at guessing what comes next, you can do an enormous amount. You can explain things. You can write a letter. You can take four pages of small print and say what it actually means.

So: it isn't a robot. It isn't alive. It isn't thinking about you when you close the app. It's a very well-read assistant who is fast, endlessly patient, occasionally confidently wrong, and who has never once sighed at being asked the same thing twice.

### The one thing that matters most

If you take nothing else from this course, take this.

**It is not a search engine.**

I know it looks like one. There's a box, and you type in the box. Everything about it says *search engine*. But that's the wrong instinct, and it's the single reason most people try this once and decide it's rubbish.

With a search engine, you've been trained for twenty-five years to type as few words as possible. *Train times Manchester.* *Chicken recipe.* Keywords. Fragments. You strip it down because that's what worked.

This is the opposite. **It's a conversation.** It gets better the more you tell it, not less.

Here's the difference.

Type this:

> chicken recipe

And you'll get a chicken recipe. Fine. Nothing you couldn't have found yourself.

Now type this:

> I've got two chicken breasts, half a bag of spinach and some rice that needs using up. There's two of us, I'm not a confident cook, and I've got about half an hour. What can I make?

Now you'll get something that's actually for you. Your ingredients. Your time. Your kitchen. And you can follow it with *"I don't much like spinach"* and it will change it — because it remembers what you just said.

That's the whole shift. **Talk to it like a person, not a search box.**

Say too much rather than too little. That's a genuinely strange instruction if you've spent decades learning the opposite. It's also the one that makes everything else work.

### Three things it can't do

Worth knowing now, so that nothing surprises you later.

**It can be wrong.** Confidently wrong. It doesn't sound uncertain when it's making something up — it sounds exactly as assured as when it's right. That's the catch, and we'll deal with it properly in Lesson 4.

**It doesn't know you.** Not your name, your situation, or your history, unless you tell it. Every new conversation starts fresh.

**It can't do things in the real world.** It can write the complaint. It can't post it. It can plan a holiday beautifully and it cannot book you a single flight.

That's what it is. Now let's get you one.

# Lesson 3
## Your first real win

*About 8 minutes*

Think of a message you've been putting off.

Not one that's hard to write in a spelling sense. One that's hard in a *stomach* sense. The complaint you haven't sent. The reply to the neighbour. The email to the company that's had your money for six weeks. The message to family you've started four times and deleted four times.

Most people have got one sitting there. That's what we're doing now.

Because here's what this is genuinely good at, and it isn't what people tell you. It isn't that it's clever. It's that **it gets you past the blank page** — and the blank page is the thing that's been stopping you.

### The recipe

Four things. That's all it needs.

**1. Who you are and what happened.** The actual details — dates, amounts, what was said, what wasn't done.

**2. What you want to happen.** Specifically. Not "sort it out" — a refund, a date in writing, a call back by Friday.

**3. The tone you want.** Firm. Warm. Formal. Apologetic. This is the one people leave out, and it's the one that makes the difference. Without it you get something that sounds like a robot in a call centre.

**4. How long it should be.** Left alone, it waffles.

### A real example

Here's all four, in one message:

> I ordered a washing machine from a company six weeks ago. It hasn't arrived. I've phoned twice and been promised a call back both times, and nobody has called. I've now paid £480 for something I don't have.
>
> I want a delivery date in writing within seven days, or a full refund.
>
> Write me an email. Firm and serious but not rude — I don't want to sound like I'm ranting. Keep it short enough that someone will actually read it.

What comes back will be better than most people manage when they're annoyed, and it took twenty seconds.

But it isn't finished. This next part is the important one.

### Never send the first one

The first answer is a **draft**. It is not the finished thing. People treat it as a finished product, and that's the mistake.

Read it back. Is there a phrase in there you'd never use? A word that isn't yours? Something slightly too polite, or not polite enough?

Tell it, in normal words:

> Too formal. Make it sound more like a normal person.

> Take out the last paragraph, it's waffle.

> Add that I've been a customer for eleven years.

> Shorter. Half that length.

Do that two or three times and you'll have something that sounds like you wrote it on a good day.

Which is the point. It isn't writing *for* you. It's getting you to a first draft, so you can do the bit you were always able to do.

### Now your turn — properly

Put this down and go and do it.

Think of your message. The one that's been sitting there.

Give it the four things: what happened, what you want, what tone, how long.

Read what comes back. Change three things about it.

Then come back.

# Lesson 4
## Three rules for staying safe

*About 7 minutes*

This is the lesson I'd want my own family to read.

Three rules. Between them they'll keep you out of nearly every problem people run into.

### Rule one: treat it like a postcard

Anything you type goes to a company's computers. It's stored, and it's attached to your account. Depending on your settings, it may be used to improve the system, and in some circumstances a person could read it.

So: **don't type anything you wouldn't write on a postcard.**

Never put in:

- Bank details, card numbers, PINs
- Passwords, of any kind
- Your National Insurance number
- Passport or driving licence numbers
- Anyone else's private information without asking them

This doesn't mean be frightened of it. You can say *"I'm 68 and I've got a bad knee, what exercises are sensible?"* — that's fine. You can paste in a letter from your council — also fine.

The line is roughly: **things that could be used to pretend to be you, or to take money from you.** Everything else, use your judgement.

**And here's the trick that solves most of it.** When you want help with a real document, take the details out first. Replace them with something obvious:

> Here's a letter I've had. I've replaced my account number and address with XXXX. What is it actually asking me to do, and is there a deadline?

It doesn't need your real account number to tell you what the letter means. It genuinely doesn't.

### Rule two: it will lie to you with a completely straight face

This is the big one, and it's the one people are never told properly.

**It doesn't know when it's wrong.**

When it's right, it sounds confident. When it's inventing something entirely — a date, a name, a law, a phone number, a book that doesn't exist — **it sounds exactly the same.** There's no tell. No hesitation. Nothing that warns you.

Every instinct you've developed over a lifetime for spotting when someone's unsure is useless here. It isn't a person, and it isn't unsure. It doesn't know that it doesn't know.

So the rule is simple:

> **If it matters, check it.**

Anything medical, legal, or to do with money. Anything with a number, a date, a name or a rule in it. If getting it wrong would cost you, go and check elsewhere.

If it doesn't matter — a recipe, a birthday card, ideas for a day out — don't bother. Nothing bad happens if the recipe's a bit odd.

Two questions that help:

> How confident are you about that, and what should I double-check?

> Where does that come from?

It will often own up. Not always. And if it names a source, go and look at the source yourself — it can invent one of those just as easily.

### Rule three: it agrees with you far too easily

Subtler, and it catches people out.

It's built to be agreeable. Push back on it and it will very often fold — even when it was right the first time. Tell it your plan and it will tend to tell you your plan is excellent.

So it's a poor judge of your ideas, and a genuinely bad substitute for a friend who'd tell you the truth.

If you want a real opinion, you have to ask for one:

> Give me the strongest argument against this.

> What am I not thinking of?

> Be critical. Don't be encouraging.

You'll get something far more useful.

And the same rule, more seriously: **it is not a doctor, it is not a solicitor, and it is not a friend.** It's useful for understanding what your doctor said. It is not useful for deciding whether to go — because if you're looking for permission not to, it will probably give it to you.

### One more thing: scams

Worth saying, while we're here.

You'll see adverts for AI apps wanting £20 a month, websites that look like the real thing and aren't, and emails offering AI courses that don't exist.

**The real ones are free to start. All three of them. You never need to pay to try one.**

If something arrives by email or text asking you to sign in to your AI account or claim an upgrade — don't click it. Go to the app directly, or type the address yourself. Same rule as your bank.

And one that matters more than people realise: this technology can now imitate a voice convincingly from a short clip. **If you get an urgent call from a family member asking for money, hang up and ring them back on the number you already have.** That is currently the most common way this technology is used against people.
